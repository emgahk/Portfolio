01 · ABOUT

A Heart for Learning, A Vision for Literacy

Highlighting

Kenn Arcenal

For Kenn, education has always been less about titles and more about impact.

Kenn Arcenal's journey as an educator and researcher is rooted in purpose, service, and a deep respect for learning. Trained initially in Foreign Service with a specialization in Diplomacy, he built early expertise through corporate training and organizational development. In 2017, driven by a desire to give back to the education sector that shaped him, he transitioned into the academe as a teacher trainer, working closely with public school teachers and students through workshops and seminars. His growing commitment to education led him abroad, where he taught at a leading university in Saudi Arabia. Recognizing the need for deeper academic grounding, Kenn later pursued graduate studies in South Korea and completed a fully funded master's degree in TESOL. He is currently pursuing a Ph.D. in English Education at Korea University under a full scholarship, refining his craft as both a scholar and educator. Kenn's research centers on literacy education, particularly reading and writing development in multilingual contexts such as the Philippines. His work highlights persistent challenges in comprehension and writing proficiency among Filipino learners, issues he believes require systematic and research- informed solutions. Among his notable achievements are Web of Science and SCOPUS-indexed publications on teacher identity and second language vocabulary acquisition, an experience that strengthened his appreciation for discipline, patience, and humility in scholarship. Beyond academics, Kenn values community, balance, and meaningful relationships. Living in Korea has allowed him to grow personally while representing Filipino educators through his everyday interactions. Looking ahead, he hopes to leave a lasting legacy through research that informs practice and policy, guided by a belief that teaching is a mission grounded in integrity, service, and a genuine love for learning.

02 · TEACHING PHILOSOPHY

I believe that education exists to help each person discover who they truly are and, in that discovery, find the courage and the reason to make the world more just.

01

Teaching Philosophy

My teaching philosophy leans toward existentialism and social reconstructionism because both have been written into my own life. I grew up with very little, and I learned early that a young person's future should never be decided by the size of their family's income or the distance of their home from the city. What changed the direction of my life was not wealth but knowledge, freely and generously shared by people who chose to believe in me before I could believe in myself. This is why the question of equity sits at the very center of my philosophy. A learner in an under-resourced public school or in a far-flung barangay is not less capable than a learner surrounded by tutors, devices, and every advantage money can buy. They are simply given less. I have come to believe that a genius or a gifted soul does not get to choose where it sprouts. Brilliant and curious children are scattered everywhere, in the mountains, in the fishing villages, and in the crowded classrooms of ordinary towns, regardless of their family's social status or surroundings. What too often decides their fate is not talent but opportunity. I see teachers as the instruments who must find these great minds wherever they are and help them flourish, not only for their own sake but so that their gifts may one day serve the betterment of society. At the same time, I want my teaching to nurture something I feel is quietly fading in this age of technology and relentless competition, which is self-awareness backed by humility and genuine compassion. It is easy today to chase achievement while forgetting how to be human with one another. I, therefore, hope to form learners who pursue excellence and self-realization without losing their kindness, who know themselves deeply and yet remain humble, and who understand that a life well lived is a life lived meaningfully alongside others. Nevertheless, even here, I remain aware of a line I must not cross. I do not wish to hand my students ready-made conclusions or shape them in my own image, but rather to place honest questions before them and trust them to decide what to believe and how to act, because a conviction imposed from the outside can never truly become their own.

02

Aim

When I look honestly at my own statement, I find that my central aim holds up well, because it asks education to form the individual and, through that individual, to help reform society. What I value most about it is that it refuses to separate the personal from the social. I do not ask students to find themselves or to serve others as if these were rival paths, since I treat self-discovery as the very thing that leads a person outward toward others. My aim is reasonable too, for I never claim that schooling will fix society, only that it can free people and prepare them to contribute. It also feels authentic to me rather than borrowed, having grown out of my own life instead of a textbook. Even so, the weaknesses are real, and I cannot pretend otherwise. I assume that a learner's freedom to define themselves will always point toward the social good, yet that outcome is never guaranteed, and a student might genuinely choose a quiet and private life instead. Placing nearly the entire burden on the teacher is another strain, because finding and lifting hidden talent is a noble task, but a single teacher alone cannot outmuscle poverty and lack of funding. My statement also speaks a great deal about characters I value such as humility and compassion while saying far less about intellectual mastery and academic rigor as goals that deserve to be pursued for their own sake. These tensions, I believe, can be reconciled, however, without abandoning what I hold dear. Rather than demanding that every learner turn their freedom toward justice, I can offer that path as an invitation, trusting that self-aware students will lean toward compassion on their own.

03

Methods

My methods have to be simple, low-cost, and doable in ordinary Philippine classrooms. That matters because I may face forty or fifty students with very few materials, so my strategies cannot depend on resources that are not there. On the existentialist side, one of the easiest strategies I can use is reflective journal writing, where students spend the last five minutes of class writing about what a lesson meant to them or how it connects to their own lives. In addition, I can use think-pair-share, which lets a quiet student first gather their thoughts, then test them with one seatmate before facing the whole room. This works well in overpopulated classes precisely because it turns a large crowd into many small, manageable conversations without needing any equipment. Oral storytelling and interviews also let learners draw on the knowledge of their families and communities, so a lesson on history or economics can begin with a grandparent's account or a neighbor's livelihood rather than an expensive textbook. Another low-cost method is peer teaching, where students who grasp a concept explain it to those who are struggling, which stretches thin resources further while quietly building confidence in both. On the social reconstructionist side, my methods can turn real community problems into learning material without any budget. A simple community-based activity might ask students to observe an issue in their own barangay, such as improper waste disposal or unsafe roads, and then discuss possible responses in class, which roots their learning in genuine civic life. Structured debates and problem-solving discussions around local concerns cost nothing but encourage them to weigh evidence, listen to opposing views, and think independently before deciding where they stand.
